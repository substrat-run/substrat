# K-42 — An impersonated operation carries both actors, and the permission model sees the impersonated one

**An impersonated operation carries both actors, and the permission model sees the impersonated one** (#868; extends K-34, whose stamping pattern it reuses verbatim). Supporting a customer's live vertical had one supported technique — asking them to screenshot things — and the usual fix is a session swap that LOSES the real actor, which is the version that fails an audit. So `getImpersonatedScope(session, principal, tenant, scope)` mints an ordinary stub with two differences. **Permission evaluation is the impersonated principal's**: the `CheckSubject` IS that principal, so `ctx.check` answers precisely what it would answer for them — support reaches what the person being helped reaches, including nothing. Intersecting with the staff actor's own permissions was considered and cannot be built: a `PlatformActorId` is branded distinctly from a `PrincipalId` (K-20) *because* a staff member is not a person in any tenant, so it holds no tuples and the intersection is empty for every key; what gates who may impersonate is therefore the mint — a platform verb, admin-logged as `impersonate` BEFORE the session exists (K-33's ordering) — not the checker. **Every record keeps both**: `impersonation` (`{ by, reason, expiresAt }`) is stamped kernel-side beside `actor` on the outbox, the denial log and the platform-intent journal, and surfaces through the kernel's timeline/history reads so a strip says *Nadia, acting as Anna* rather than *Anna*. Absent from `DomainEventInput` exactly as `authorization` is, so module code can neither claim a session nor drop the one it is in; `ctx.impersonation` is readable so a handler can REFUSE under one. Sessions are **bounded** (`expiresAt`, defaulted and capped at `IMPERSONATION_MAX_MINUTES`, enforced per INVOKE rather than at the mint) and **reason-carrying** (required by the schema). Both adapters implement it, held together by a contract suite; on the Cloudflare one the DO acknowledges that it recorded the session and the coordinator refuses the SUCCESS without that acknowledgement — the #129/#116 skew pattern, applied to the one field whose loss is invisible in the response.

## Why

The expensive halves were already built — per-person staff actors (K-20), the kernel-stamped authorization chain (K-34), the admin log, the access log — and what was missing was a seam, not a mechanism. Building it now costs a day; building it after five customer projects means retrofitting a session concept through every door that mints a stub. The two rules pull in opposite directions and both are load-bearing: evaluating as the impersonated principal is the only way the answer means anything (an answer computed with staff authority is not "what Anna sees"), and recording only that principal would make the trail say a customer did what support did — the same laundering the actor union refuses for connectors, where a third member exists precisely so the spine can say "Scrive did this" without naming a human. Keeping the session on the RECORDS rather than in a separate session table is what makes it survive a scope export, a restore and a fork, and what makes "who was really behind this write" answerable from the row itself rather than from a join nobody remembers to make.
